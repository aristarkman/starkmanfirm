Slip and fall in NJ: proving the owner had notice

Falling is not a case. Proving the owner knew about the hazard — or should have — is the case. And most of that proof is gone before the ambulance leaves the lot.

Published October 15, 2026 · Morris Starkman

Know what you actually have to prove

People come in believing the fall itself is the claim. It is not. A New Jersey property owner is responsible only if it had notice of the dangerous condition and did nothing about it in time.

Our Supreme Court put it plainly in Jeter v. Sam's Club, 250 N.J. 240 (2022): the owner is liable "if the owner had actual or constructive knowledge" of the condition. The same opinion carries the sentence that ends a great many cases — the "[e]xistence of an alleged dangerous condition is not constructive notice of it." The puddle being there does not prove the store should have known about it.

  • Actual notice: somebody who worked there knew. An employee saw it, a customer reported it, it had been written up before.
  • Constructive notice: it had been there, in the words of the Court, "for such a length of time as reasonably to have resulted in knowledge and correction" if the owner had been reasonably diligent.

Understand that constructive notice is an argument about time

Almost every contested fall case comes down to one question: how long was it there? Nobody has a stopwatch, so the answer gets built out of circumstances. Jeter says constructive notice can be inferred from eyewitness testimony or from the "characteristics of the dangerous condition" itself.

That is not abstract. It means things like:

  • Dirt, cart tracks or footprints through a spill — somebody walked through it before you.
  • A dried or tacky edge on a liquid, which takes time to form.
  • Produce that is browned or flattened rather than fresh.
  • A stair tread, handrail or mat worn or broken in a way no one did that morning.
  • The store's own sweep log or inspection sheet, showing the last check was two hours ago.

Every one of those is a photograph.

Photograph it before anyone cleans it up

A spill gets mopped in ten minutes. Ice gets salted. A broken step gets a work order. None of that is sinister — it is what a decent property manager does — but it destroys your case if nobody recorded the scene first. Before you leave:

  • A wide shot showing where the hazard sits in the aisle or the walkway.
  • A close shot with something for scale — your phone, a shoe, a quarter.
  • The edges of the spill, the tracks through it, whatever is floating in it.
  • The ceiling, cooler or display above it, if the source matters.
  • The absence of a cone or wet-floor sign. Photograph the empty floor around it.
  • The lighting, if it was dark, and the shoes you were actually wearing.

Then report it to a manager, get a name, and ask for the incident report number. Write down names and phone numbers of anyone who saw it, including the employee who came over — they are strangers you will never find again otherwise.

Ask for the video in writing, the same week

Most stores have cameras, and most overwrite within weeks. A written request that the footage, the sweep logs and the inspection records for that shift be preserved — sent to the store and its insurer with the date, time and location — is one of the cheapest things a lawyer does and one of the most valuable. An empty or missing log sheet tells a jury something by itself.

Do not count on the mode-of-operation rule

New Jersey has a narrow exception that relieves a customer of proving notice at all. Where a self-service business operates in a way that makes a hazard foreseeable as a matter of probability, the plaintiff gets an inference of negligence. The mode-of-operation rule requires three things: a self-service setting, an accident in an area affected by that operation, and a "reasonable factual nexus" between the self-service activity and the hazard.

The rule is real but narrower than people assume. In Nisivoccia v. Glass Gardens, Inc., 175 N.J. 559 (2003), it applied: the store sold grapes in open, vented bags that permitted spillage, so loose grapes near the checkout were foreseeable. In Jeter, nineteen years later, the same fruit gave the opposite answer — the grapes came in sealed clamshell containers, and the Court held that selling them that way "does not create a reasonably foreseeable risk that grapes will fall to the ground." Same aisle, same grapes, different packaging, different case. Assume you have to prove notice.

Find out who owns the sidewalk before you count on suing

New Jersey draws a hard line here. Under Stewart v. 104 Wallace Street, Inc., 87 N.J. 146 (1981), commercial owners are liable for injuries on the sidewalk abutting their property. Under Luchejko v. City of Hoboken, 207 N.J. 191 (2011), residential owners are not; the Court declined to extend sidewalk liability to a 104-unit condominium. A residential owner can still be liable for creating or worsening a hazard, but not for simply failing to clear one. What decides it is whether the property's predominant use has the capacity to generate income.

Treat a fall on public property as a different case with a 90-day clock

If you fell in a school, a municipal lot, a county park or a NJ Transit station, the Tort Claims Act governs and the deadlines are brutally short.

  • Notice of claim within 90 days of accrual — N.J.S.A. 59:8-8. Miss it and you are barred, subject only to N.J.S.A. 59:8-9: a Superior Court judge may permit late notice within one year, on a motion and affidavit showing "extraordinary circumstances," and only if the entity has not been substantially prejudiced.
  • A higher standard of fault. You must show the entity's conduct was palpably unreasonable — plainly and obviously without a reasonable basis. Carelessness is not enough.
  • A damages threshold. Under N.J.S.A. 59:9-2(d), no pain and suffering is awarded against a public entity except for permanent loss of a bodily function, permanent disfigurement or dismemberment, where medical treatment expenses exceed $3,600.

Expect your own conduct to be on trial

The carrier's first theory is almost always that you were not watching where you walked. New Jersey applies modified comparative negligence: under N.J.S.A. 2A:15-5.1 your damages are reduced by your own percentage of fault, and if your fault is greater than the defendant's you recover nothing. That is why the recorded statement matters, and why "I guess I wasn't really looking" is worth thinking about before you say it.

The deadline for a fall on private property is two years from the injury — N.J.S.A. 2A:14-2. Plenty of time to file; nowhere near enough to recover evidence that was mopped up on day one.

Questions we get

If I fell in a store in New Jersey, is the store automatically responsible?

No. A New Jersey property owner is liable only if it had actual or constructive notice of the dangerous condition and failed to correct it in time. As the Supreme Court said in Jeter v. Sam's Club, 250 N.J. 240 (2022), the existence of a dangerous condition is not by itself constructive notice of it.

What is constructive notice in a New Jersey slip and fall case?

Constructive notice means the hazard was present for such a length of time that a reasonably diligent owner would have discovered and corrected it. It is usually proved circumstantially — dirt or footprints tracked through a spill, a dried edge on a liquid, browned produce, refrozen ice, or the store's own sweep log showing the last inspection.

What should I photograph after a fall?

The hazard wide and close, something for scale, the edges of the spill and anything tracked through it, the source above it, the absence of any warning cone or sign, the lighting, and the shoes you were wearing. Then report it, get the incident report number, and take the names and phone numbers of witnesses before they leave.

Who is responsible if I fall on a sidewalk in New Jersey?

It depends on whether the abutting property is commercial or residential. Under Stewart v. 104 Wallace Street, Inc., 87 N.J. 146 (1981), commercial owners are liable for injuries on the abutting sidewalk. Under Luchejko v. City of Hoboken, 207 N.J. 191 (2011), residential owners are not, unless they created or worsened the condition.

How long do I have to file a slip and fall claim in New Jersey?

Two years from the injury for private property, under N.J.S.A. 2A:14-2. If you fell on public property, you must serve a notice of claim within 90 days under N.J.S.A. 59:8-8, and a judge may allow late notice only within one year on a showing of extraordinary circumstances.
