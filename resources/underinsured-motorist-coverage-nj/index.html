Underinsured motorist coverage in NJ: the number that matters most

The other driver's insurance pays first. Yours pays what is left, up to your limit. If you never looked at that limit, look at it before you need it.

Published November 1, 2026 · Morris Starkman

Find the underinsured number on your declarations page

Most people know their liability limits. Almost nobody knows the line below it: uninsured/underinsured motorist coverage, often written "UM/UIM." It pays you when someone else causes the crash and cannot cover what you lost. In a serious injury case, it is often the largest check available.

New Jersey sets a floor. Under N.J.S.A. 17:28-1.1, a standard policy issued or renewed on or after January 1, 2026 must carry at least $35,000 per person and $70,000 per accident for bodily injury. A basic policy is excepted from that requirement. That floor is thin. Plenty of hospital stays cost more.

Know when a driver counts as underinsured

The statute gives a precise test. A vehicle is underinsured when the total liability limits available to the at-fault driver are "less than the applicable limits for underinsured motorist coverage" on your policy. The comparison is limit against limit. It is not your damages against their policy.

Two consequences follow:

  • If you carry $35,000 and the other driver carries $35,000, that driver is not underinsured. You have no UIM claim, however badly you were hurt.
  • If you carry $100,000 and the other driver carries $35,000, that driver is underinsured.

The test also requires that the at-fault driver's bodily injury limits "have been exhausted by payment of settlements or judgments." You collect from them first.

Do the subtraction before you celebrate

The statute also says your UIM limit "shall be reduced by the amount he has recovered" from the at-fault driver's liability coverage. Here is a made-up example, not a prediction:

  • You carry $100,000 in UIM coverage.
  • The other driver carries $35,000 and the insurer pays it.
  • Your UIM coverage is $100,000 minus $35,000. That leaves $65,000.

Your total from both policies tops out at $100,000, no matter what the jury says your harm was worth. The state's Department of Banking and Insurance puts it the same way: your claim cannot exceed the difference between your limit and the other driver's.

Raise your liability limit to raise your UIM limit

You cannot buy UIM coverage above your own liability coverage. The statute says UM/UIM limits "shall not exceed the insured's motor vehicle liability policy limits." Insurers must offer UM/UIM up to at least $250,000 per person and $500,000 per accident on a standard policy, but never higher than your liability limits.

So the way to protect yourself is to raise the liability limit. That feels backwards. You are buying coverage that protects the other person. It also lifts the ceiling on the coverage that protects you.

Do not count on stacking

In some states you add the coverage on each car. Not here. N.J.S.A. 17:28-1.1(c) says UM/UIM coverage "shall not be increased by stacking" the limits of multiple vehicles on one policy, or the limits of multiple policies. If more than one policy applies, recovery cannot exceed the higher of the limits, and the policies share it in proportion. A second car does not double your coverage.

Tell your insurer before you settle

This is where valid claims die. The at-fault driver's insurer offers its limit. You are tempted to take it and move on. Do not accept until your own carrier has been told.

The statute makes UIM coverage subject to policy terms on "unauthorized settlements" and subrogation, and the Supreme Court has enforced the point. In Ferrante v. New Jersey Manufacturers Insurance Group (N.J. Apr. 11, 2018), the driver sued the at-fault driver and never told his own carrier. He was never given a chance to step in. The Court held that because of the "complete absence of notice" over years of litigation, the carrier could refuse to pay UIM benefits.

The rule is simple:

  • Give your carrier written notice when you file suit against the at-fault driver.
  • Give written notice of every settlement offer before you accept it.
  • Give notice of any arbitration, high-low agreement or trial date.

The carrier can then pay you the offered amount and take over your claim against the other driver, or let you settle. Either way, you keep your claim. That is why it matters who is handling this for you.

Check the pieces that change the claim

  • Your lawsuit option. The statute ties the tort option you elected to non-economic loss in uninsured motorist claims. Ask how it applies to yours, and read our limited vs. unlimited guide.
  • A business-owned car. If a company's policy covers the vehicle you were driving, an employee cannot receive less UM/UIM coverage than the named insured. Ask for the company's declarations page.
  • Property damage. UM/UIM property damage carries a $25,000 aggregate limit on plans issued or renewed on or after January 1, 2023, with the first $500 excluded.
  • Hit-and-run. A hit-and-run vehicle is treated as an uninsured vehicle, which makes this coverage the one that pays.

Changes are pending in Trenton, including a bill that would make UM/UIM compulsory. It has only been introduced. Plan around the law as it stands today.

Questions we get

What is underinsured motorist coverage in New Jersey?

It is coverage on your own policy that pays when the driver who injured you has liability limits lower than your underinsured motorist limits. Under N.J.S.A. 17:28-1.1, a standard policy issued or renewed on or after January 1, 2026 must include at least $35,000 per person and $70,000 per accident.

How does New Jersey decide whether the other driver is underinsured?

By comparing limits, not damages. A vehicle is underinsured when the at-fault driver's total liability limits are less than the underinsured motorist limits on your policy, and those liability limits have been exhausted by settlement or judgment. If both of you carry $35,000, there is no underinsured claim.

How much will my underinsured motorist coverage pay?

Your limit is reduced by what you recover from the at-fault driver's liability coverage. With $100,000 in UIM coverage and $35,000 recovered from the other driver, $65,000 remains.

Can I stack underinsured motorist coverage in New Jersey?

No. N.J.S.A. 17:28-1.1(c) says UM/UIM coverage is not increased by stacking the limits of multiple vehicles on one policy or multiple policies. Your UM/UIM limit also cannot exceed your own liability limits.

Do I have to tell my insurer before I settle with the at-fault driver?

Yes. In Ferrante v. New Jersey Manufacturers Insurance Group (N.J. Apr. 11, 2018), the Supreme Court held that a driver who gave his carrier no notice of the litigation or the proceedings could be denied UIM benefits. Give written notice of the lawsuit and of every settlement offer before you accept.
