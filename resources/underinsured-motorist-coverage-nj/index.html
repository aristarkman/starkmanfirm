Underinsured motorist coverage in NJ: the number that matters most

The other driver's insurance does not decide whether you are covered. Your own underinsured limit does. Check that one number before you need it.

Published October 15, 2026 · Morris Starkman

Know what underinsured coverage pays

You are hurt by a driver who has insurance. Their policy is too small for your injuries. Underinsured motorist coverage, "UIM," is the part of your policy that fills the gap.

The state's Department of Banking and Insurance puts it this way: it pays you when the at-fault driver "is insured, but who has less coverage than your underinsured motorist coverage." Note the comparison. It is their limit against your limit. It is not their limit against your damages.

Understand the limits-to-limits test

The statute is N.J.S.A. 17:28-1.1(e). A vehicle is underinsured "when the sum of the limits of liability under all bodily injury and property damage liability bonds and insurance policies available" to the at-fault driver is "less than the applicable limits for underinsured motorist coverage" on your policy.

Then the statute subtracts. Your UIM limit "shall be reduced by the amount he has recovered under all bodily injury liability insurance or bonds."

  • You carry $250,000 of UIM. The other driver carries $50,000. Your damages are $400,000. You collect the $50,000, then up to $200,000 from your own carrier.
  • You carry $35,000 of UIM. The other driver carries $50,000. That driver is not "underinsured," whatever your damages are. Your UIM pays nothing.

The Department's own example runs the same way: $100,000 of UIM, a driver with $35,000, $45,000 in damages. The at-fault insurer pays $35,000 and the injured driver's insurer pays the other $10,000.

See how it can go wrong at work

In Chiaccheri v. Zurich American Insurance Co., decided by the New Jersey Supreme Court on August 6, 2026, a worker was hurt driving the employer's vehicle. The employer's policy had $2,000,000 in liability coverage but only $15,000 of UIM. The at-fault driver carried $100,000. The insurer denied the claim because $100,000 is more than $15,000.

The Court held that, for an employee, the maximum UIM coverage under N.J.S.A. 17:28-1.1(f) is the UIM limit the employer chose for itself, not the policy's liability limit. A low limit on a work vehicle can sink an injured employee's UIM claim. If you were hurt driving for work, tell us. Workers' compensation may also apply.

Find the number on your declarations page

Look for "Uninsured/Underinsured Motorist" on your policy. You have one of three situations.

  • A Basic Policy. It has no UM or UIM coverage. The statute exempts it, and the Department's comparison chart lists UM/UIM under the Basic Policy as "None."
  • The minimum. For policies issued or renewed on or after January 1, 2026, the required floor is $35,000 per person and $70,000 per accident.
  • Higher limits. Under N.J.S.A. 17:28-1.1(b) an insurer must offer UIM up to $250,000 per person and $500,000 per accident. Your UIM limit cannot exceed your own liability limits.

That last rule is why you should raise your liability limits first. UIM is capped by them.

Do not count on stacking

Some states let you add the UIM on each car together. New Jersey does not. Under N.J.S.A. 17:28-1.1(c), UIM coverage "shall not be increased by stacking the limits of coverage of multiple motor vehicles covered under the same policy" or by "multiple policies available to the insured." Insuring three cars does not triple your protection.

Tell your carrier before you settle

This is where good UIM claims die. New Jersey courts require you to keep your own insurer informed while you pursue the at-fault driver.

  • When you sue the other driver, notify your UIM carrier. The Supreme Court required that in Zirger v. General Accident Insurance Co., 144 N.J. 327 (1996).
  • When the other driver's insurer offers its limit, notify your carrier in writing before you accept. This is the "Longworth letter," from Longworth v. Van Houten, 223 N.J. Super. 174 (App. Div. 1988), approved in Rutgers Casualty Insurance Co. v. Vassas, 139 N.J. 163 (1995).
  • Your carrier then chooses: pay you the amount of the offer and take over your claim against the other driver, or let you settle.

The reason is simple. Your carrier has the right to recover what it pays from the at-fault driver. The courts call that subrogation. A settlement or judgment you reached without telling it can destroy that right, and the court will not make the carrier pay for your silence.

Skip the letter and you can lose the coverage. In Vassas the insured sued and took an arbitration award without telling the carrier. He was barred from UIM benefits. In Ferrante v. New Jersey Manufacturers Insurance Group, decided April 11, 2018, the Supreme Court held a carrier could refuse UIM benefits after years of missing notice, without proving it was harmed.

Never sign a release or cash a settlement check from the other driver's insurer until your UIM carrier has answered in writing.

Questions we get

What is underinsured motorist coverage in New Jersey?

It is coverage on your own auto policy that pays when the at-fault driver is insured but carries lower liability limits than your underinsured motorist limit. Your insurer pays the gap, up to the difference between your limit and theirs, under N.J.S.A. 17:28-1.1.

How does New Jersey decide whether a driver is underinsured?

By comparing limits, not damages. Under N.J.S.A. 17:28-1.1(e), a vehicle is underinsured when the liability limits available to the at-fault driver are less than the underinsured limits on your policy. Your limit is then reduced by what you recover from their insurer.

Does a New Jersey Basic Policy include underinsured motorist coverage?

No. N.J.S.A. 17:28-1.1(a) excepts the Basic automobile insurance policy, and the Department of Banking and Insurance lists uninsured/underinsured coverage as none under the Basic Policy. It is part of the Standard Policy.

Can I stack underinsured motorist coverage in New Jersey?

No. N.J.S.A. 17:28-1.1(c) provides that UIM coverage is not increased by stacking the limits of multiple vehicles on one policy or of multiple policies available to you.

Do I have to tell my insurer before I settle with the at-fault driver?

Yes. New Jersey courts require notice when you sue and when you receive a settlement offer, so your insurer can protect its right to pursue the other driver. Failing to give notice can bar your underinsured claim, as in Rutgers Casualty Insurance Co. v. Vassas, 139 N.J. 163 (1995).
