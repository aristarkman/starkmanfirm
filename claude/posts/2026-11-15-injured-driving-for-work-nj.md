# Injured driving for work in NJ: workers' comp, a car claim, or both

> A crash on the job can open two claims: workers' compensation and a lawsuit against the other driver. The order you handle them in matters.

## Ask first whether the trip counts as work
Workers' compensation covers injuries "in the course of employment." Driving to your regular
job usually is not. N.J.S.A. 34:15-36 says employment begins when you arrive at the employer's
place of employment to report for work, and ends when you leave it. But the same section makes
exceptions. One is for an employee "who utilizes an employer authorized vehicle," whose
employment runs with "the authorized operation of a vehicle on business authorized by the
employer."
The Supreme Court applied that rule in *Keim v. Above All Termite & Pest Control*
(N.J. Nov. 21, 2023). Keim drove a company truck from home to job sites. One morning he clocked
in, saw he was short on supplies and headed to the shop to restock. He was hit on the way. The
Court held that he was covered, and gave a two-part test:

- The employer authorized the vehicle for you to operate.
- You were operating that vehicle for business the employer expressly authorized.

The Court also said the rule does not apply every time you drive an authorized vehicle, and
does not cover a worker who is "merely" commuting. Run an errand for your boss and you may be
covered. Drive straight to the first stop of the day and the answer is harder.

## Let workers' comp pay the medical bills, not your auto policy
New Jersey auto policies carry personal injury protection, or PIP, which pays medical bills
whoever caused the crash. When you are hurt working, the statute sends those bills elsewhere.
N.J.S.A. 39:6A-6 says benefits "collectible under workers' compensation insurance" shall be
deducted from the PIP benefits otherwise collectible. In plain terms, comp pays first.
That has a practical edge. Do not assume your auto carrier will pick up what comp should have
paid. If the employer denies the claim, ask us before you decide which carrier to bill.
Give your employer notice of the injury within 14 days. N.J.S.A. 34:15-17 gives limited relief
up to 30 days and, with a good excuse, up to 90 days. After 90 days without notice or
knowledge, "no compensation shall be allowed." You then have two years from the accident to file
a claim petition with the Division of Workers' Compensation (N.J.S.A. 34:15-51), or from the last
payment if the carrier has paid.

## Know that you can still sue the other driver
Comp does not turn on proving fault. N.J.S.A. 34:15-7 says it is paid "without regard to the
negligence of the employer." If someone else caused the wreck, you can pursue them as well, and
that lawsuit is where a claim for pain and suffering lives. N.J.S.A. 34:15-40 says
your right to compensation "shall not operate as a bar to the action of the employee." The
general deadline for a personal injury lawsuit is two years, according to the New Jersey Courts.
Do not sit on that claim, because the comp carrier can take it over. Under 34:15-40(f), if you
neither settle nor sue within one year of the accident, the carrier can send a written demand.
Ten days later it may settle with the at-fault driver or sue. That settlement or judgment
"shall constitute a bar to any further claim or action" by you against that driver. The carrier
is looking out for its own reimbursement, not for your pain and suffering.

## Expect the comp carrier to claim part of your recovery
If you recover from the other driver, the carrier gets repaid for what it paid you. Under
34:15-40(b), it is reimbursed for medical expenses and compensation paid, "less employee's
expenses of suit and attorney's fee." Those deductions are capped. Expenses count up to $750.
The attorney's fee counts up to 33 1/3% of the part the carrier is reimbursed.
If your recovery is smaller than what the carrier owes you, subsection (c) makes the carrier
liable for the difference. Do not settle the car claim without a lawyer who has run those
numbers against your comp file.

## Prepare for the verbal threshold
The third-party case is a car accident case, and the usual auto rules apply. If your policy
carries the limitation on lawsuit option, N.J.S.A. 39:6A-8(a) bars a claim for pain and suffering
unless your injury falls in one of six categories:

- Death.
- Dismemberment.
- Significant disfigurement or significant scarring.
- A displaced fracture.
- Loss of a fetus.
- A permanent injury within a reasonable degree of medical probability, other than scarring or disfigurement.

## Know where the courts have not settled the question
Whether the no-fault auto law blocks a comp carrier from suing the at-fault driver was
contested. In *New Jersey Transit Corp. v. Sanchez* (N.J. May 12, 2020), the Supreme
Court was equally divided and left the Appellate Division's ruling for the carrier in place, so
there is no binding Supreme Court opinion. If a carrier is pursuing the other driver, ask your
lawyer how that affects you.
